# Paper Summaries

This directory contains structured, original summaries of research papers studied throughout the AI/ML learning journey.

The goal is to develop the ability to understand research contributions, explain technical ideas clearly, evaluate experimental results, identify limitations, and connect research concepts to practical AI/ML implementations.

Summaries should be written in your own words rather than copied from abstracts, papers, or other summaries.

## What to Include

Paper summaries may cover research from:

- Machine Learning
- Deep Learning
- Natural Language Processing and Large Language Models
- Generative AI
- Computer Vision
- Reinforcement Learning
- AI Agents and Multimodal AI
- Model Optimization, Evaluation, and Deployment

## Recommended Summary Structure

Use the following template for each paper.

### 1. Paper Information

- **Title:**
- **Authors:**
- **Publication Year:**
- **Conference or Journal:**
- **DOI or arXiv ID:**
- **Original Paper Link:**
- **Official Code Repository:**
- **Research Category:**

### 2. Problem Statement

Explain the problem the paper addresses.

Describe:
- What problem exists?
- Why is it important?
- What limitations existed in previous approaches?
- What motivates the proposed research?

### 3. Main Contribution

Explain what the authors contribute and what is new compared with earlier work.

Distinguish between the paper's actual contributions and your own interpretation of their significance.

### 4. Core Concepts

Explain the background concepts required to understand the paper.

Include relevant:
- Mathematical concepts
- Algorithms
- Model architectures
- Terminology
- Assumptions

Link to related notes elsewhere in the repository when useful.

### 5. Methodology and Architecture

Describe how the proposed method works.

Include:
- Model architecture or algorithm
- Input and output
- Main processing steps
- Training objectives or loss functions
- Important equations
- Diagrams, when useful and properly attributed

Explain the purpose of each important component instead of merely listing technical terms.

### 6. Experimental Setup

Record the experimental conditions where available:

- Datasets
- Data preprocessing
- Training and validation strategy
- Baseline methods
- Hyperparameters
- Evaluation metrics
- Hardware and computational requirements

### 7. Results and Findings

Summarize the important findings.

Explain:
- Which metrics improved?
- How did the method compare with baselines?
- Were the improvements meaningful?
- Which claims are directly supported by the experiments?

Avoid reporting numbers without explaining what they measure.

### 8. Strengths and Limitations

**Strengths**
- What does the method do well?
- Which ideas are particularly useful?
- Where could the method be applied?

**Limitations**
- What assumptions does the method make?
- What failure cases or weaknesses remain?
- Are there reproducibility or computational concerns?
- What questions remain unanswered?

### 9. Personal Understanding

Write your own explanation of the paper.

Consider answering:
- What is the central idea in simple language?
- What did I learn from this paper?
- Which concepts were difficult to understand?
- How does this paper connect to earlier research?
- What would I investigate next?

### 10. Practical Implementation

When appropriate, record:
- Official implementation
- Important libraries and dependencies
- Steps needed to reproduce a result
- A smaller experiment suitable for learning
- Possible project applications
- Differences between the paper's method and your implementation

Not every paper needs a full implementation. Focus on reproducing or testing the central idea when it is feasible and educationally useful.

### 11. Related Research

Link to papers that:
- Introduced the original concept
- Improved or extended the method
- Challenged the paper's conclusions
- Applied the approach to a different domain

### 12. Final Takeaways

Conclude with a concise summary of:

- The main idea
- The most important contribution
- The key limitation
- The practical significance
- The next concept or paper to study

## Suggested File Organization

```text
Paper-Summaries/
├── README.md
├── paper-title-one.md
├── paper-title-two.md
└── paper-title-three.md
```

The filenames above are examples, not required names. Preserve existing filenames where practical. Use the paper's title or a recognizable abbreviated title to make files easy to locate.

For a large collection, you may introduce topic-specific subfolders later, but avoid unnecessary nesting while the collection is small.

## Research Reading Workflow

1. Read the abstract and introduction to understand the research problem.
2. Review the figures, tables, and conclusion for an initial overview.
3. Study the methodology and important equations.
4. Examine the experiments, baselines, and evaluation metrics.
5. Review limitations and related work.
6. Write the summary from your understanding, using the paper as a reference.
7. Verify technical details and citations against the original source.
8. Record follow-up questions and possible experiments.

## Quality Guidelines

- Explain concepts clearly without sacrificing technical accuracy.
- Define unfamiliar terminology.
- Use equations when they improve understanding, and explain the symbols.
- Include diagrams only when they add value and attribution requirements are satisfied.
- Cite the original paper for technical claims and results.
- Clearly distinguish the authors' findings from personal opinions.
- Do not invent results or imply that an experiment was reproduced when it was not.
- Update summaries when important corrections or follow-up research become available.

## Copyright and Attribution

Research papers, figures, code, and datasets may have different copyright and licensing restrictions.

Prefer linking to authorized paper versions and official implementations. Do not redistribute restricted PDFs or reuse figures without appropriate permission or licensing. Write original explanations and cite the authors and sources.
